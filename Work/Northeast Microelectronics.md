### Tell us about something you are especially proud of:

At the end of my freshman year, I would've answered this question with a final project for one of my courses: a bespoke iPod clone fitted with a PCB I designed from scratch. But after teaching that same course this summer, I now think of my students' projects and the seven weeks spent creating them.

Every half-week, PHYS S-12 students learn a new digital fabrication skill and apply their knowledge by designing, programming, and fabricating a project. Projects range from laser-cut cardboard kinetic sculptures to 3D-printed cycloidal gearboxes and Cartesian pen plotters. After developing a broad range of digital fabrication skills, students culminate the course with a project of their own choosing. Seeing students go from having never having written code before to building their own ESP32-powered smart water bottles is incredibly rewarding. Even for students already comfortable with programming, introducing them to fabrication felt more than worthwhile. I especially enjoyed teaching an extension school student, who came from a career in software startups, the intricacies of CAD and the careful steps required to avoid getting everything sticky while using a resin 3D printer. Laughing with students at 12 AM as we recut a severely undersized chair leg for their plywood armchair on our desktop router is probably one of my favorite memories from the lab.

That same enthusiasm for fabrication also led me to share one of my own hobbies with the class: PCB design and milling. My favorite machine in the lab is our Roland SRM-20 desktop mill, which I use to quickly fabricate simple PCBs without having to wait for overseas board houses. When I took the class, being the only student with experience using KiCad, I was the only person who milled a PCB for their CNC assignment. Determined to change this in the summer, I hosted a weekend-long KiCad workshop, filmed a YouTube tutorial, and individually trained interested students to use the mill and its accompanying CAM software. In the end, around a third of the class chose to create a PCB for their assignment. Seeing one of my interests resonate with so many students left me with an immense sense of fulfillment.

But the students weren't the only ones who grew over the summer. I went from being nervous to even speak in front of my lab section to knowing each student's aspirations, hobbies, and the projects they were most excited to build. And, in many ways, I was simply filling the shoes of the mentor who first inspired to continue making things in college. It was incredibly gratifying to have the opportunity to become that person for someone else. I hope my students carry the same curiousity and ambition into college as they continue to tinker and make the occasional mess along the way. Their growth has become one of the things I'm most proud of, and I hope the things I've taught them will stay with them long after the seven weeks we spent together this summer.

### If you have done an internship or research/class-project related to STEM, electronics, or related area, please let us know about it here:

This summer, I conducted time series forecasting research on semiconductor manufacturing processes at Northeastern University's Advanced and Intelligent Manufacturing Systems (AIMS) Lab under the supervision of Dr. Xiaoning Sarah Jin.

Traditional methods for detecting tool anomalies, predicting machine failures, and estimating process outcomes remain largely vendor locked and insensitive to subtle drift patterns. To address these issues, the AIMS lab is building a tool-agnostic system that leverages novel developments in AI/ML to better monitor every process in the semiconductor fab. At the heart of this system is a time series foundation model capable of predicting sensor data across manufacturing runs. 

Recurrent neural networks and long short term memory models have long been the standard for time series forecasting, but both struggle with the lengthy historical sequences. This pitfall is critical in semiconductor fabrication, where monitoring long term dependencies like machine health and consumable wear is essential. My research focuses on leveraging the context aware transformer, the de facto architecture for LLMs, for use in time series. 

I developed a Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder (VQVAE) based tokenizer and byte pair encoding (BPE) token compression algorithm to predict CMP sensor data. First, raw time series data is encoded into a sequence of vectors. Each of these vectors is then matched to the closest vector from a learned, finite codebook. Using the indices of the codebook vector as our token ID, we've transformed our continuous sensor data into a discrete sequence of IDs that can then be fed into a transformer! Combined with byte pair encoding, a strategy typically used in natural language processing to generate subword codebooks, we can compress time series data by a factor of more than 10.

Even at one sample per second, a modest context window of 10,000 tokens (frontier models regularly advertize context windows in the millions of tokens) can capture and accurately represent over 24 hours of data. To help with data scarcity and compute bottlenecks, I've created semi-synthetic datasets and self-deployed NixOS SSH servers, making GPU-accelerated training possible on my laptop anywhere with an internet connection. Now, I'm moving onto developing a more generalized model and implementing multivariate forecasting.
