An phrase you'll hear around campus is the “Harvard introduction”. When meeting other students, especially during the first few weeks of class, it's common to introduce yourself with your year, house, and concentration. For me, and many other students, the Harvard introduction is the immediate answer that comes to mind when asked "who are you?".

Allegiances are the pilliar of identity that is easiest to recognize. I don't harbor any remarkable beliefs nor do I practice a religion. I'm not particularly tied to any ritual in my life, even if I celebrate Christmas or my birthday every year. Worse, values like "freedom" feel too vague to wholeheartedly brand myself with. Allegiances, on the other hand, are objective: it's an indisputable fact that I'm a Harvard Sohpomore studying computer science staying in Mather house. The straightforwardness of allegiance explain why they're the first thing we introduce ourselves with. Each of characteristic is a components of my identity, but you could probably fill Harvard Hall 101 with Mather computer science concentrators. The remaining pillar of identity, emotionally meaningful experiences, stands out to me the most.

Growing up with an identical twin brother is a unique experience that has undoubtedly shaped who I am today, but I don't often bring up having an identical twin brother in conversation, let alone within the first 30 seconds of meeting someone. Even my roommate, who I share almost every meal with and talk to for hours on end, only learned I had a brother when he visited Harvard right before the start of Winter break. Yet after thinking carefully about "who am I?", being a twin seems essential.

I'm a very competitive person. I struggle to stop myself from comparing myself with others, take video games or extracurricular competitions a little too seriously sometimes, and have an tendency to bite off more than I can chew. I'd argue that this personality trait didn't develop in a vacuum. Consciously or not, as children, twins compete for our parents attention. We experience every milestone together, which breeds competition more than we'd both like to admit. Even my own interests are shaped by my brother's. One of my allegiances, computer science, is partially due to my brother, who first took an interest into robotics and engineering. My current plan of study wavers as he describes the interesting things he's learning as a Electrical and Computer Engineering student.

As counterintuitive as it seems, the person I am today has probably been shaped by my brother more than me. I'm certain my experience of growing up with a twin brother manifests itself in other ways, some of them probably too subtle for me to notice. Even in college, while we're seperated by hundreds of miles, he's the both the first person I celebrate victories with while also being first person I turn to in a crisis.
